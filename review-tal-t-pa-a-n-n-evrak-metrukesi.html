Political

Talât Paşa'nın Evrak-ı Metrukesi

by Murat Bardakçı
★★★★☆

Bardakçı grounds this deep dive into Talât Paşa's life and the Armenian relocation controversy in original archival documents, complete with translations for wider accessibility. It's not a comprehensive history, and the old Turkish vocabulary can be tough going, but for anyone seriously studying this transitional period, it's an important read.

Murat Bardakçı·January 2026

If you have read my previous reviews of history books, you know that my main interest lies in the pre-Republican history of the Turks. I was never particularly interested in the post-Republican era of our history. It always felt too recent to me, and because of that, it did not truly capture my attention. This was something I wanted to change.

This book was one of the steps I took to remedy that gap in my reading. I chose Murat Bardakçı specifically because he consistently works with primary sources that he can physically prove and document. His approach feels grounded in archival material rather than interpretation alone. More importantly, he is willing to criticize both sides of Turkey’s political discourse instead of fully aligning himself with one camp. That perceived objectivity is the main reason I chose this book.

This book contains a large collection of documents related to Talât Paşa’s life and actions. More importantly, it delves deeply into one of the most controversial chapters of our history, namely the Armenian relocation. Bardakçı openly criticizes both the public and the political class in Turkey for being inadequate when it comes to countering genocide-related arguments. Rather than relying on emotional or ideological responses, he highlights the lack of serious engagement with archival material. In that sense, this book is especially valuable for anyone genuinely trying to educate themselves on the Armenian relocation issue.

The author builds his arguments almost entirely on official documents taken from Ottoman and Republican archives. I understand that this topic is controversial, but it is part of our history, and avoiding it does not make it disappear. Bardakçı includes the original documents in the book, meaning that if you can read Ottoman Turkish written in the Arabic alphabet, you are able to examine the sources yourself. At the same time, he also provides modern Turkish translations of these documents, making the material accessible to a wider audience.

My main criticism here is related to language. Bardakçı frequently uses old Turkish vocabulary in his own commentary, which can be challenging for readers like me who are only fluent in modern Turkish. I think the book would have benefited greatly from including modern Turkish equivalents alongside these older terms. This would have helped younger readers fully grasp every detail and engage more confidently in discussions surrounding this topic.

That said, this book is not meant to be a wide or fully comprehensive history lesson. Its main focus is the documents themselves and their translations. If you are looking for a broad, structured overview of late Ottoman and early Republican history, this book is not that. However, that does not mean you should skip it. On the contrary, it is an important work and one that definitely deserves a place on your reading list if you are interested in the recent history of Turkey, especially the transitional period between the Ottoman monarchy and the early years of the Republic.

The book concludes with an interview conducted with Talât Paşa’s widow. Since she was very old at the time and the interview was conducted only a few years before her passing, the conversation occasionally drifts away from the main topic. Still, Murat Bardakçı does a good job of steering it back in the right direction and keeping it relevant.

What I appreciate most about books like this is how they humanize historical figures. More often than not, when we read history in school, these people feel distant and abstract. They exist as names, dates, and titles rather than as real human beings. Books like this bridge that gap. They present historical figures not just as political actors, but as people with personal lives, relationships, and contradictions. That humanization is one of the strongest aspects of this book.

My rating for this book is 3 out of 5. This score is mainly due to the fact that the book is not a comprehensive history work and because there is a clear need for more consistent modern Turkish translations, which the book lacks. That said, if you are interested in this topic and genuinely want to educate yourself about the early Republican era and the transitional period from the Ottoman Empire to the Republic of Turkey, this book is a must-read.

By Ruşen M. Özoruç
