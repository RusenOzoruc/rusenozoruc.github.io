Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind

by Yuval Noah Harari
★★★☆☆

Sapiens is an accessible, fast-moving introduction to human history, but it's less a neutral survey of prehistory than one historian's opinion piece, marked by selective criticism of religions and a curiously cynical take on modern progress. Rereading it years later, what once felt insightful now reads as a book with a clear, if unstated, agenda.

Yuval Noah Harari·July 2026

Here's the review with em dashes removed:

This isn't my first encounter with this book. I read it about ten years ago, at eighteen, too young, I think now, to grasp its full scope. A decade later, I decided to give it another shot.

But I want to be upfront about something: I went into this reread skeptical. Given recent events and the ongoing genocide of the Palestinian people, I looked into the author himself. What I found was unsettling. While Harari has been openly critical of the current Israeli government, he has remained silent on the genocide of Palestinians, instead framing his position as wanting to "take back" Zionism from a government he says has given it a bad name by turning it into a fascist ideology. That silence matters to how I read this book now. Sapiens was written years before the genocide, so this reread became something more than nostalgia. It became a test. I wanted to see how Harari's predictions and arguments hold up now that the stakes are no longer theoretical.

It's worth noting that Harari himself is not a scientist but a historian, at least according to the "About the Author" section of the book itself. Why does this matter? Because his arguments come from a historian's perspective, not a scientist's. He's making predictions and telling us the history of our species based on the information available to him. And history, as we know, is often written by the winners. It's a discipline that's always shifting, always being revised. We're constantly uncovering new evidence that may or may not disprove the very arguments laid out here.

So what is the book actually trying to do? Its goal is to explain complex issues and the history of our species in language almost anyone can understand. It doesn't matter if you've never read a book like this before, or never had any interest in the topic, this is about as beginner-friendly as it gets. The prose moves fast, the chapters are short, and Harari has a real gift for landing a big idea in a single memorable sentence. You won't struggle to follow the arguments or the history. In that sense, the book does its job flawlessly. But it's attempting something enormous: explaining the entire known history of humankind in roughly 500 pages, split into four parts, the Cognitive Revolution, the Agricultural Revolution, the Unification of Humankind, and the Scientific Revolution. Is covering all of that possible in one book? No. Does the book manage it anyway? Sort of. The information here stays surface-level by necessity. If a topic in this book grabs your interest, you'll need to seek out deeper, more specialized reading, this book alone won't get you there. To its credit, though, it never claims otherwise. This is a book for people who know nothing about our history to walk away knowing at least something.

So why did I give this book 3 stars, if it achieves exactly what it sets out to achieve? Because achieving your stated goal isn't the same as achieving it well.

First, the book needs to be split into two parts: the first is genuine prehistory, backed by evidence, really just the Cognitive Revolution section; the other three sections (Agricultural Revolution, Unification of Humankind, Scientific Revolution) are essentially recent history you could find in any other history book. The Industrial Revolution, for instance, eats up a significant chunk of the page count, alongside a heavy dose of theory-crafting. I personally enjoy theory-crafting, and there's plenty of it here. But if you're marketing your book as a history of prehistoric Sapiens, and only roughly a quarter of it actually delivers that, it's going to frustrate readers who came for what was promised.

More than that, Harari regularly inserts his own musings and personal opinions throughout the book. This is not an objective work. Especially in the first half, he acts less like a historian and more like a moderator of r/atheist, quick to criticize, quick to mock. And here's what I noticed: he's perfectly comfortable ridiculing Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, and others, but Judaism is conspicuously untouched. Why is that? If you're an atheist, the logical position is that all religions are equally baseless, not "all religions except mine." I'll admit there's a more generous reading here, maybe it's simply his own cultural background he's less inclined to interrogate, rather than a deliberate ideological blind spot. But even granting that, the pattern is hard to unsee once you notice it, especially combined with his public stance on Zionism. It's the kind of double standard that made me genuinely angry reading this book.

On top of that, his take on the modern world compared to our hunter-gatherer ancestors is downright cynical. The vibe I get from him is that, according to Harari, our prehistoric ancestors were mentally and physically superior to us, despite almost all the evidence, and all the statistics he himself includes in this book, saying the opposite. His reasoning seems to boil down to: they worked fewer hours and didn't sit at a desk all day. He argues that the moment humans settled down and started the Agricultural Revolution, we doomed ourselves, and that this is somehow the root cause of modern depression. I mean, come on. In some parts of the book, it almost feels like he's disappointed that infant mortality has gone down and that more people today are obese than starving. I understand you're fond of Zionism, but babies not dying and people not starving shouldn't make you this sad. And to be clear, the statistics on decreased infant mortality and decreased starvation that I'm citing in this review are pulled straight from his own book. My guy looked at these numbers and actively chose to be cynical and nihilistic about them.

To be fair, he does make some good points when criticizing current agricultural practices and our treatment of animals, points I actually agree with. But there's something almost funny about it: without the very practices he criticizes, and the Agricultural Revolution he seems to resent so much, he wouldn't be here to write this book in the first place.

Would I recommend Sapiens? If you've never read a history or science book in your life, yes, it's an accessible on-ramp, and it will make you think, even when you disagree with it. But go in knowing it's not the neutral, prehistory-focused overview it's marketed as. It's one historian's opinion piece dressed up as a survey of human history, and the further you get from the Cognitive Revolution, the more that becomes obvious. Rereading it at this point in my life, and at this point in history, I liked it a lot less than I did at eighteen, not because the writing got worse, but because I got better at spotting what it was actually doing.
