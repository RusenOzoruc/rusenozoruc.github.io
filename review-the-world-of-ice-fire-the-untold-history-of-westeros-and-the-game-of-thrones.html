The World of Ice & Fire: The Untold History of Westeros and the Game of Thrones

by George R.R. Martin , Elio M. García Jr. , Linda Antonsson
★★★★★

The World of Ice & Fire isn't a novel, it's a masterclass in worldbuilding, presented as a biased in-world history text written by a maester loyal to the Baratheon regime. Dense, chronological, and packed with detail, it achieves in one volume what many other franchises need multiple books to accomplish.

George R.R. Martin , Elio M. García Jr. , Linda Antonsson·January 2026

This book is not a novel. Do not expect a traditional plot, a character journey, or a clear protagonist and antagonist. There are no main characters or side characters in the conventional sense, and there is no emotional arc to follow. This book reads like a history book set in the world of A Song of Ice and Fire, and because of that, you do not form emotional connections to the figures mentioned in the same way you do in the main series.

This is very much a book for hardcore fans of the series. That said, I actually think that if you are considering getting into A Song of Ice and Fire and the wider fandom, you can start with this book. Since there is no central plot or character-driven narrative, it serves as a broad and informative foundation rather than a demanding emotional commitment.

I am a huge fan of A Song of Ice and Fire. It is the series that got me into fantasy and dark fantasy in general, which is exactly where I think ASOIAF belongs. I had already read the main series before picking up this book, and this is not my first time reading The World of Ice & Fire. I had read it before, but for the sake of this review, I decided to reread it. And honestly, boy oh boy, I enjoyed it.

As a worldbuilding enthusiast, this book is a masterclass in worldbuilding. It is written from the perspective of a maester living during the reign of Tommen I. Because of that, the text is not fully objective. There are clear undertones of propaganda throughout the book. Since it is written during the Baratheon era, Robert Baratheon is often portrayed as a heroic savior who freed Westeros from the cruelty of the Targaryens.

If you have read the main series, there is a certain irony to this portrayal. As readers, we know just how damaging Robert’s reign truly was for Westeros. Seeing him framed as a heroic figure makes the book even more enjoyable, because it mirrors how real-world history is often written by the victors, not by those who suffered the consequences.

Instead of being a flaw, this bias pulls you deeper into the world. It feels like a real historical document shaped by politics, propaganda, and limited perspective, which fits perfectly within the setting.

One thing I really liked is that the maester frequently cites other books written by different maesters across Westeros. You are not reading a single, definitive version of history, but a collection of interpretations, secondhand accounts, and sometimes questionable sources. This mirrors how real history is recorded and preserved, and it adds a great deal of depth and credibility to the world.

This is probably the most comprehensive worldbuilding book I have read to date. I have also read other major worldbuilding-focused books, such as the World of Warcraft: Chronicles series and lore books from the Diablo franchise. While those are impressive in their own right, this book achieves in a single volume what many other franchises needed multiple books to accomplish. That is really saying something.

Even though the book looks like it is around 350 pages long, the amount of written text and information packed into it easily equals a 700-page book. There is an incredible density of lore, history, and detail on almost every page.

The artwork in this book is also fantastic. It is detailed, atmospheric, and, most importantly for me, consistent. Consistency in visual worldbuilding matters a lot, and this book absolutely delivers on that front. The illustrations feel like they belong to the same world, the same history, and the same cultures.

You learn about the Targaryen kings and their reigns in full detail. You learn about the world before the Targaryens ever arrived in Westeros. You learn about Westeros before the Andals, and you are introduced to every major culture that exists within A Song of Ice and Fire. Nothing feels rushed or surface-level. Each section adds another layer to the world.

Another thing I really appreciated is that everything is presented chronologically. The book is not all over the place. Events, eras, and dynasties are laid out in a clear historical order, which makes an enormous difference for a book this dense.

That said, the book can become very heavy with text at times, and there were moments where it felt demanding to read. However, if you enjoy reading history or worldbuilding-focused books, this should not be an issue at all. In fact, that density is part of what makes this book so rewarding.

Even though I read this book after finishing the five main novels, I would actually recommend anyone who is thinking about starting the series to read this book first. There are so many Easter eggs, references, and historical callbacks in the main series that you simply will not fully grasp without this context. Reading this book first makes those moments land harder and feel more intentional, and it deepens the entire experience.

If you are worldbuilding your own setting, and if you are a worldbuilding enthusiast like me, this book is essential reading and a true masterclass in worldbuilding. There are countless tiny details throughout the book that make the world feel rich, believable, and alive, and there is so much to learn from them. For me, perfection is not about grand ideas alone, but about small things done correctly. This book is full of those small, carefully considered details.

My final score for this book is 5 out of 5. I fully understand that I might not be entirely objective here, considering I am a huge fan of George R. R. Martin and his work. That said, this score reflects my genuine experience with the book and what it set out to achieve. As a worldbuilding resource and a fictional history, it does exactly what it promises, and it does it exceptionally well.

By Ruşen M. Özoruç
