Nuclear War: A Scenario

by Annie Jacobsen
★★★★★

Nuclear War: A Scenario reads like a nonfiction thriller, walking through a minute-by-minute chain of events that could end civilization while seamlessly explaining the real systems and terminology behind it. Terrifying, meticulously researched, and impossible to put down, it's one of the most important books of our time.

Annie Jacobsen·November 2025

I first heard of Annie Jacobsen through various podcasts, where her deep research and storytelling immediately caught my attention. After learning about her work, I added all of her books to my reading list but decided to start with her most recent one, Nuclear War: A Scenario. Given the current state of the world, it felt like the right choice. Living in Türkiye, I am almost in the middle of everything that has been happening: a NATO country that hosts nuclear weapons on its soil, bordered by the Syrian civil war where chemical weapons have been used, and just north of us, the ongoing conflict between Russia and Ukraine. Surrounded by such realities, I felt this was a subject I needed to be informed about, at least to have some surface-level understanding of how nuclear conflict might actually unfold.

But calling this book a surface-level introduction to the subject would be selling it short. Nuclear War: A Scenario gives you everything you need to know, and more. It does not read like a research paper or a dry policy document, nor does it feel like a simple newspaper article. Instead, it reads almost like a nonfiction thriller. As the title suggests, Jacobsen presents the topic through a single, minute-by-minute, almost second-by-second scenario, a chain of events that could realistically lead to the end of the world as we know it. She masterfully blends factual information with storytelling, creating a narrative that is both terrifying and impossible to put down. The result is a book that feels as informative as it is cinematic, and as educational as it is emotionally gripping.

Many might dismiss works like this, much like governments did during the Cold War when those who warned about nuclear fallout and the dangers of nuclear weapons were labeled as alarmists. But I do not see it that way. This is not paranoia, it is reality. As the book itself suggests, we are one major misunderstanding away from nuclear annihilation. At one point, I had to take a long break from reading. I think it was my mind’s way of disassociating from the grim reality this book presents. It shattered my illusion of safety, that comforting belief that someone, somewhere, must have everything under control. After reading this, that illusion was gone. It also broke whatever trust I still had in the world’s nuclear authorities. The thought that such unimaginable destruction rests on systems run by flawed human beings was simply too much, and I needed time to step away before I could finish the book.

The testimonies from the survivors of Nagasaki and Hiroshima chilled me to my core. At times, they almost made me physically sick, not because of the book itself, but because of what they revealed about the way we destroy each other. When we read about wars in history books or hear about them in the news, we only ever see numbers: thousands dead, cities leveled. But what we do not see, or what is often kept from us, is how those people actually died. The pain, the radiation burns, the screams, the aftermath. Hearing the firsthand testimonies of survivors opened my eyes to the true horror of nuclear weapons. It stripped away the abstraction and made me confront the reality of what those words, nuclear strike, fallout, mutual destruction, actually mean for human beings.

What I liked most about this book is how Jacobsen handles complex information. While we are following the unfolding scenario, whenever a technical term, institution, or military concept appears, she takes a step back and gives us the context we need to understand it. She does not assume the reader already knows the terminology of nuclear command structures or defense systems. Instead, she pauses the story, explains the background clearly, and then pulls us right back into the narrative. As a result, with each chapter, you find yourself becoming more and more knowledgeable about the subject, not just about nuclear weapons, but about the systems, people, and decisions behind them. It is a rare skill to teach and terrify the reader at the same time, and Jacobsen manages to do both flawlessly.

The pacing of this book is absolutely top-notch. Once you start, it is almost impossible to put it down. Despite being nonfiction, it reads with the intensity of a political thriller. Every chapter ends with a sense of urgency, and you constantly find yourself wondering what is going to happen next, even though deep down you already know how the story will end. That is what makes it so haunting. Jacobsen builds tension not through fiction, but through the terrifying reality of how fast and easily such a catastrophe could unfold. It is rare for a nonfiction book to grip you like a novel, but this one does exactly that.

If I have to offer any criticism, I am really cherry-picking here. To be honest, the section where Jacobsen discusses the aftermath of the war could have been longer and more detailed. I found myself wishing for a deeper look at what the world, or what is left of it, might look like after such a catastrophe. But in truth, that probably comes more from my sadness that the book was ending than from any real flaw in her writing. Besides, diving too deeply into theorizing about the aftermath might have shifted the book toward speculative fiction and taken away from the brutal realism that makes it so powerful. So, I completely understand the author’s decision to keep that part concise.

After finishing this book, I could not stop thinking about how fragile our 12,000-year-old civilization truly is, how everything humanity has built could vanish in a matter of minutes because of a misunderstanding, a miscommunication, or the incompetence of people in power. It is terrifying to realize that our survival depends not just on technology or deterrence, but on human judgment, something that has failed countless times throughout history. This book forced me to confront that uncomfortable truth. It reminded me that we are not as safe, advanced, or rational as we like to believe. One mistake, one ego, one bad decision, and the entire story of human civilization could end before we even understand what happened.

Overall, Nuclear War: A Scenario is probably one of the most important books of our time, especially in this era of proxy wars and rising global tensions. It should be translated into every major language, particularly in countries that possess or host nuclear weapons, and it should be mandatory reading in schools. After all, this is not fiction or fearmongering; it is reality and a possible future we must do everything in our power to prevent. Annie Jacobsen has written a book that not only informs but also awakens. If you know little or nothing about this subject, by the time you finish, you will be as informed as many of the experts themselves.

By Ruşen M. Özoruç
