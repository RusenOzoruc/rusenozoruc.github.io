The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy #1

by Douglas Adams
★★★☆☆

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy is packed with inventive worldbuilding and irony, but its constant absurdity sometimes overshadowed the story for me. Marvin the depressed robot was the highlight, his gloom making perfect sense in such a chaotic universe.

Douglas Adams·September 2025

I’ve never read any of Douglas Adams’ work before. I was probably too young to encounter him when these books first came out, but I’ve always heard his name since many people consider him part of the great sci-fi classics. That curiosity finally pushed me to give The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy a try.

I should mention that I read this book in Turkish. I couldn’t find an English copy at the time and didn’t want to wait any longer. Still, I plan to reread it in English when I can, because I have the feeling some of the humor and wordplay might be much sharper in the original.

To be honest, I came away a little disappointed. From what I’ve seen in other reviews, people seem very divided about Adams: you either love his style or you can’t stand it. I think I’m somewhere in the middle. There is no doubt the book is packed with creative worldbuilding, but the humor often overshadowed those ideas for me. Arthur loses his entire planet and it’s brushed aside with jokes. That reaction didn’t feel believable. Of course, I don’t expect the sci-fi elements themselves to be realistic, but I do expect the characters’ emotions to make sense.

The plot itself felt chaotic and difficult to follow at times. The constant randomness pulled me out of the story instead of pulling me in. There were moments when I struggled with the motivation to keep going because it felt like the narrative had no clear direction.

Maybe there are philosophical undertones to Adams’ writing that I missed. It’s possible the Turkish translation didn’t carry them across, or maybe I just didn’t pick up on them. What I did notice, though, was the irony, and I think those moments were handled really well.

As for the characters, Arthur is a good anchor as the confused ordinary man stuck in the middle of everything. Ford adds a lot of energy, though sometimes it felt like his role was more about comic relief than depth. Zaphod Beeblebrox was wild and entertaining, but also a little too chaotic for me to enjoy fully. Marvin the depressed robot ended up being my favorite. His gloomy perspective in such an absurd universe somehow made perfect sense.

Despite my criticisms, I can’t deny Adams’ originality. Not everyone can be this inventive. The writing style was also easy to read, even in translation, which made the book accessible despite the parts I didn’t enjoy.

After seeing that this is just the beginning of a longer series, I don’t think I’ll continue anytime soon. I want to clear my head with some grimdark fantasy and cyberpunk first. Still, I do plan to give Adams another chance in the future. Next time, I’ll make sure to read his work in English to get the full effect, and I’d recommend others do the same if possible.

If you enjoy comedy-driven sci-fi that embraces absurdity and doesn’t worry too much about making sense, this book is probably perfect for you. But if you prefer serious, darker worlds where the worldbuilding and technology at least try to be logical, then you may not enjoy it as much.

By Ruşen M. Özoruç
